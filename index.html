The problem

The technology is not the hard part. Knowing what to do, and what it returns, is.

Every board has asked, and every management team has said yes. That decision was made some time ago.

What is missing is a defensible answer to a narrower question. Of everything a company could do, which projects can this one actually execute, and what does each one return?

That is a judgment about the business — where the hours go, which work repeats, what a point of margin is worth — rather than about the technology. It requires someone who has carried a P&L.

What we answer

Three questions, in this order.

First

What to do

A ranked list of projects the business can execute, with the reasoning set out so it can be challenged — and the ones to leave alone.

Second

What it returns

What each initiative is worth, and how long before it reaches the numbers: weeks, a quarter, or a year. An initiative measured at eighteen months has no one accountable for it and rarely arrives.

Third

How to build it

The sequence, what to buy and what to build, where a person stays in the loop, and who owns it. This is where most plans stall, so we will run it where needed, until each initiative is live and someone inside has taken it over.

The answer usually involves no new software. Migration is the most expensive purchase available to a mid-market company, and an AI layer over the systems already in place returns in weeks rather than years.
