Position

The scarce input is not knowledge of the technology. It is judgment about the business.

Boards have asked the question and management teams have answered it. Conviction is no longer in short supply, and neither is familiarity with what the tools can do.

What remains difficult is knowing which initiatives will survive contact with a real business — real staff, real exceptions, real customers on the other end — and what each one actually takes to finish. The principals spent thirty years building and running companies before advising anyone on one. That experience is the product.

What operating experience changes

Four things that follow from having run the business.

01

Knowing what actually works

A demonstration proves a capability. It says nothing about whether the thing survives a Tuesday in a real company, with real staff, real exceptions and real customers on the other end.

02

Knowing how hard it is

Every initiative has a cost that does not appear in the vendor's estimate: the integration nobody scoped, the exceptions, the training, the six weeks of adoption. Estimating that honestly is most of the value of the exercise.

03

Respect for process

Nothing durable gets built on top of a process nobody has written down. Where the process is sound, the work is straightforward; where it is not, that is the finding, and it comes first.

04

Separating myth from reality

A great deal of what is claimed for this technology is true, some of it is true only in a demonstration, and a little of it is simply wrong. Sorting the three is a judgment about businesses, not about models.

Both principals also invest, through OVD Capital. A business is therefore examined the way an owner examines one, with the same question in mind: what would be worth doing here if this were ours?

The mandate

Three questions, in sequence.

First

What to build

A ranked schedule of initiatives the business can execute, the reasoning set out so that it can be examined, and an explicit account of what should be left alone.

Second

What it returns

The value of each initiative and the interval before it reaches the accounts, whether that is weeks, a quarter or a year. An initiative measured at eighteen months has no accountable owner and rarely arrives.

Third

How it is built

Sequence, the distinction between what to purchase and what to construct, the points at which human review is retained, and ownership. Most programs stall here, which is why the firm will run the work where a company has no capacity to.

The answer seldom involves new software. Migration is the most expensive purchase available to a mid-market company, whereas an AI layer over the systems already in place returns in weeks rather than years.

Engagement structure

Engagements begin narrowly and extend as the evidence warrants.

  1. 01 AI Opportunity Assessment Interviews, process mapping, a ranked opportunity matrix and a ninety-day plan. Fixed scope and fixed fee. 2–3 weeks
  2. 02 Program direction A single external owner of execution, responsible for the pilots, the tooling, vendor management and reporting on a fixed cadence. Part-time and continuous is more effective than full-time and episodic. Monthly
  3. 03 Implementation Construction of the initiatives that matter, with the people required to complete them, and a documented handover that leaves no continuing dependency. Per initiative
  4. 04 Capital Where the fit is right and both parties wish it, a conversation about investment follows, through OVD Capital. A separate firm, a separate agreement, and never the stated purpose of an engagement. Where warranted
Clients

Two kinds of client, one question.

Private equity

Portfolio companies rarely lack conviction. They lack selection and capacity.

  • Selection — a ranked schedule of initiatives a portfolio company can realistically execute, and an explicit account of what to leave alone.
  • Comparability — identical method and scoring in each company, so that opportunities rank across the book rather than within a single holding.
  • Execution — sequence and ownership, and direct delivery where the company has no capacity of its own.
