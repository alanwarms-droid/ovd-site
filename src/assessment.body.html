The engagement

The AI Opportunity Assessment

Two to three weeks inside the business. It ends in a ranked list of what to do, what it's worth, what it costs, and the order to do it in. Fixed scope, fixed fee.

Why it exists

Most AI advice surveys the technology. An owner needs to know which two things to fund.

Every leadership team we meet has the same problem. They know AI matters. They have read the same articles as everyone else, tried a few tools, and possibly run a workshop. What they do not have is a defensible answer to a narrow question: given this business, these people, and these systems, where is repetition costing us real money, and what would it take to stop it?

That question can be answered in three weeks. It does not require a platform, a data warehouse, or a twelve-month program. It requires talking to the people who do the work, reading what the company has already written down, and having enough operating experience to tell a genuine opportunity from an interesting demo.

We do this the way we would if we owned the business — because in some cases, we go on to.

How it runs

Three weeks, four movements.

Week one

Interviews

Leadership first, then the people actually running the work — operations, sales, finance, whoever carries the load. Thirty to sixty minutes each. We are listening for where the same thing gets done twice.

Week one–two

Process and systems

We map the recurring processes end to end and inventory the systems underneath them — what is licensed, what is used, what people quietly work around.

Week two

What you've already tried

Tools evaluated, experiments run, prototypes built by someone on the team. This is usually where the head start is, and it is almost always underused.

Week three

Prioritisation

Every opportunity scored on value, cost, complexity, risk, and time to impact — then sorted into tiers, with the reasoning shown.

What you get

A document you can act on without us.

Written to be circulated to a board, not to justify a follow-on engagement.

  • The processes reviewed Each recurring process, the tools in use, and the friction we observed or your team already documented.
  • The opportunities Every AI and automation opportunity we found, grouped by theme, each with an implementation sketch — not a concept, a first move.
  • The prioritisation matrix Value, cost, complexity, risk, and time to impact for each one, sorted into tiers with the reasoning visible so you can disagree with it.
  • A ninety-day path What to start in week one, what to pilot, who owns it, and what success looks like for each.
  • Where to go deeper Honest notes on further discovery, vendors worth evaluating, and where a prototype would settle a question faster than more analysis.
What we need

Less than you'd expect.

  • Thirty to sixty minutes each with the leaders we agree on, plus whoever else you think is worth hearing.
  • Whatever process documentation exists — SOPs, system overviews, templates, sample reports. If none exists, that is a finding, not a problem.
  • Examples of repetitive work: manual workarounds, communication templates, reporting that gets rebuilt by hand.
  • A list of the software the business runs on.
  • Anything you have already tried on AI, including what didn't work.
  • Availability for short clarifying calls during the review period.

We do not need broad system access to start. If reviewing live systems or data becomes useful, we agree the appropriate level of access and the confidentiality terms around it first.

What we do with what we learn

Everything you share is confidential and used only for the assessment and for discussing what follows. Nothing goes into a case study, a website, or a conversation with anyone else without your written say-so.

Afterwards

No obligation, in either direction.

The assessment does not commit you to an initiative, a vendor, or a further engagement, and it does not commit us to implementation work. Plenty of companies should take the document and run it with their own people. If that is you, we will say so.

Where it makes sense to keep working together, it usually takes one of three shapes: a program driver retainer, where one of us owns execution day to day; implementation of the two or three initiatives that matter most; or, occasionally, capital — where we like the business enough to invest in it. Each is a separate conversation and a separate agreement.

Fees are fixed and agreed before we start, scaled to the scope rather than the size of the company. Ask and we will tell you on the first call.
