The engagement

The AI Opportunity Assessment

Two to three weeks inside the business, concluding in a ranked schedule of what to do, what each item is worth, what it costs, and the order in which the work should be done. Fixed scope, fixed fee.

Why it exists

Surveying the technology is the easy half. An owner needs to know what to fund.

Most leadership teams arrive at the same position. The technology plainly matters, the same articles have been read, a few tools have been tried and a workshop may have been held. What is absent is a defensible answer to a narrow question: given this business, these people and these systems, where is repetition costing real money, and what would it take to stop it?

The question can be answered in three weeks. It requires no platform, no warehouse and no twelve-month program. It requires conversations with the people performing the work, a reading of what the company has already documented, and sufficient operating experience to distinguish a real opportunity from an interesting demonstration.

The business is examined the way an owner would examine it. In some cases the principals go on to become one.

How it runs

Three weeks, four movements.

Week one

Interviews

Leadership first, then the people running the work in operations, sales and finance. Thirty to sixty minutes each. The object is to find where the same thing is done twice.

Week one–two

Process and systems

The recurring processes are mapped end to end and the systems beneath them inventoried: what is licensed, what is used, and what people quietly work around.

Week two

Prior attempts

Tools evaluated, experiments run, prototypes built by someone on the team. This is generally where the head start lies, and it is almost always underused.

Week three

Prioritization

Every opportunity scored on value, cost, complexity, risk, and time to impact, then sorted into tiers with the reasoning set out.

Deliverables

A document that can be acted on without the firm.

Written to be circulated to a board, not to justify a follow-on engagement.

  • The processes reviewed Each recurring process, the tools in use, and the friction observed or already documented internally.
  • The opportunities Every AI and automation opportunity identified, grouped by theme, each with an implementation sketch rather than a concept.
  • The prioritization matrix Value, cost, complexity, risk, and time to impact for each, sorted into tiers with the reasoning set out so it can be challenged.
  • A ninety-day path What to start in week one, what to pilot, who owns it, and what success looks like for each.
  • Where to go deeper Where further discovery is warranted, which vendors merit evaluation, and where a prototype would settle a question faster than more analysis.
Requirements

Less than might be expected.

  • Thirty to sixty minutes with each of the agreed leaders, and with anyone else management considers worth hearing.
  • Whatever process documentation exists: standard procedures, system overviews, templates, sample reports. Where none exists, that is itself a finding.
  • Examples of repetitive work: manual workarounds, communication templates, reporting rebuilt by hand each period.
  • An inventory of the software the business runs on.
  • Anything already attempted, including what did not work.
  • Availability for short clarifying calls over the review period.

Broad system access is not required at the outset. Where reviewing live systems or data becomes useful, the appropriate level of access and the confidentiality terms are agreed first.

Treatment of information

Everything disclosed is confidential and used only for the assessment and for discussing what follows. Nothing appears in a case study, on a website or in conversation with any third party without written consent.

Afterwards

No obligation, in either direction.

The assessment commits the client to no initiative, vendor or further engagement, and commits the firm to no implementation work. Many companies should take the document and run it with their own people. Where that is the case, the firm will say so.

Where continuing makes sense, it ordinarily takes one of three forms: a program direction retainer, under which one of the principals owns execution day to day; implementation of the initiatives that matter most; or, occasionally, capital, where the principals regard the business sufficiently highly to invest in it. Each is a separate conversation and a separate agreement.

Fees are fixed and agreed before work begins, scaled to scope rather than to the size of the company. The figure is given on the first call.
