For private equity

You know AI belongs in the portfolio. Your companies have nobody to build it.

We go into a portfolio company, work out the two or three initiatives worth starting, and then build them. Same method every time, so the answers compare across your book — and the work actually ships, rather than joining the pile of plans nobody had capacity to run.

The problem

The commitment was the easy part. Capacity is what is missing.

Nobody in your portfolio needs persuading. The IC has asked, the LPs ask every annual meeting, and every management team has said yes. So each one produces something — a workshop, a pilot, a licence purchase, a slide with four workstreams on it. A year later the honest answer to "what changed in the P&L" is often nothing.

Two things are missing, and the second is the real problem. First, a company facing a dozen plausible options has no reliable way to choose among them — management proposes what management wants to build, vendors propose what vendors sell, and the consultancies produce a maturity model.

Second, and more fatally: even with the right list, nobody has the hours. Mid-market portfolio companies are lean by design. The CFO is doing three jobs, there is no internal AI lead and no budget line for one, and the operating partner has twenty companies to cover. So the plan lands on people who already have full-time jobs, and quietly stops.

Advice is abundant and getting cheaper by the month. Someone who will actually build the thing, inside a business with no spare capacity, is not.

How we choose

What moves an initiative to the top of the list.

Six tests we apply to every candidate. The ones that pass go first; the ones that fail two of them are usually a year away from mattering.

  • It's repetition, not invention The same judgment made hundreds of times a month, by people, from information that already exists somewhere in the business. If it happens twice a quarter, automating it is a hobby.
  • The data already exists and someone already trusts it If the plan opens with "first we clean the data," it is a data project wearing an AI costume. Those take a year and get cancelled in month eight.
  • A human can stay in the loop The initiatives that ship are the ones where AI drafts and a person approves. Full autonomy is where pilots go to die — and where the liability lives.
  • It rides the systems already in place Anything requiring a CRM or ERP replacement moves payback from weeks to years, and dies in change management long before that. Migration is the most expensive thing a mid-market company can buy.
  • Someone inside already wants it Adoption is the binding constraint, not capability. An opportunity nobody asked for does not ship, however good the business case looks.
  • You can measure it inside a quarter If success is defined at eighteen months, no one will be accountable for it and no one will be surprised when it doesn't arrive.
Where it stalls

Four ways good programs lose a year.

Licences distributed without workflow change. Most portfolios have already bought seats for everyone and watched nothing move. That is not a tooling problem, and more seats will not fix it — the work itself has to change shape, which is a management job rather than a purchase.

Customer-facing AI in relationship businesses. Where the relationship is the product, the client should feel the results of AI — speed, precision, recall — and never the machinery. This is the single most common way a portfolio company damages itself with good intentions.

Anything that needs the warehouse built first. Sometimes genuinely necessary. Almost never the place to start, and frequently a way of deferring the hard conversation about what would actually be done with it.

The platform with AI attached. Vendors are adding capability to the systems your companies already run. Riding their roadmap captures most of the value of "new software" at none of the migration cost. Buy where the vendors are close; build only where the advantage is proprietary.

Across the book

The value compounds when you run it more than once.

A single assessment tells a management team what to do. Running the same assessment in three or four companies tells you something more useful: where the portfolio's real operating upside is concentrated, which opportunities recur across companies and could be built once, and — not least — which management teams can actually execute.

Because the method and the scoring are identical each time, the outputs are comparable. You can rank opportunities across the book rather than within one company, and put capital and attention where the return is, instead of where the loudest CEO is.

How it runs

  • Two to three weeks per company. Interviews with leadership and with the people doing the work, a process and systems map, and a review of what the company has already tried.
  • Every opportunity scored on value, cost, complexity, risk, and time to impact, then tiered — with the reasoning shown so your team can disagree with it.
  • A ninety-day path with named owners and one success metric per initiative.
  • Fixed fee and fixed timeline, agreed before we start.

Afterwards

We stay until it works.

The pattern that works is a single outside program driver — part-time but consistent — who runs the pilots, builds or configures the tools, manages the vendors, and reports on a fixed cadence to you and to the CEO. Part-time and consistent beats full-time and episodic, and both beat a plan handed to a team with no capacity to run it.

We stay until each initiative is live and a named person inside the company has taken it over. Where a portfolio company can genuinely run it themselves, we will say so and hand it across — but that is the exception, and pretending otherwise is how these programs die.

Co-investment

Where we like a business enough to put our own money in, we will — and we are equally happy to be the operating partner on a deal that is yours. We invest our own capital, deal by deal, with no fund and no clock. We are not competing with you: our own investing sits well below institutional scale.

On live deals

We do occasionally look at a target during diligence — whether the AI narrative in the CIM is real, and what operating upside is genuinely available post-close. It is the same work against a shorter clock. Ask if it's useful; it isn't what most funds come to us for.
