For private equity

You know AI belongs in the portfolio. The question is what to do, and what it returns.

We go into a portfolio company and come back with a ranked list of projects that are genuinely doable there, what each returns and how fast, and the ones to leave alone. Same method every time, so the answers compare across your book. Then, where the company has no capacity to build it, we do that too.

The problem

The commitment was the easy part. Choosing well is not.

Nobody in your portfolio needs persuading. The IC has asked, the LPs ask every annual meeting, and every management team has said yes. So each one produces something — a workshop, a pilot, a license purchase, a slide with four workstreams on it. A year later the honest answer to "what changed in the P&L" is often nothing.

The difficulty is that a company facing a dozen plausible options has no reliable way to choose among them, or to say what any of them is worth. Management proposes what management wants to build. Vendors propose what vendors sell. Operating partners are generalists being asked to referee a technical question. The consultancies produce a maturity model.

Then there is a second problem, which arrives once the list is right: mid-market portfolio companies are lean by design. The CFO is doing three jobs, there is no internal AI lead and no budget line for one, and you have twenty companies to cover. A sound plan lands on people who already hold full-time roles, and stops there.

The scarce skill is knowing which of twelve credible options is worth a quarter of somebody's attention, and what it returns. The scarce resource, right after that, is anyone with the hours to build it.

How we choose

What moves an initiative to the top of the list.

Six tests we apply to every candidate. The ones that pass go first; the ones that fail two of them are usually a year away from mattering.

  • It's repetition, not invention The same judgment made hundreds of times a month, by people, from information that already exists somewhere in the business. Work that recurs twice a quarter does not repay the effort of automating it.
  • The data already exists and someone already trusts it A plan that opens with "first we clean the data" is a data project. Those run for a year and are typically cancelled before they finish.
  • A human can stay in the loop The initiatives that ship are the ones where AI drafts and a person approves. Full autonomy is where the pilots stall, and where the liability sits.
  • It rides the systems already in place A CRM or ERP replacement moves payback from weeks to years, and is usually lost to change management well before then. Migration is the most expensive purchase available to a mid-market company.
  • Someone inside already wants it Adoption is the binding constraint, not capability. An initiative nobody asked for does not ship, however strong the business case.
  • You can measure it inside a quarter Where success is defined at eighteen months, no one is accountable for it and it rarely arrives.
Where it stalls

Four ways a sound program loses a year.

Licenses distributed without workflow change. Most portfolios have already bought seats for everyone and seen nothing move. This is not a tooling problem and additional seats will not resolve it. The work itself has to change shape, which is a management task rather than a purchase.

Customer-facing AI in relationship businesses. Where the relationship is the product, the client should feel the results of AI — speed, precision, recall — and never the machinery. This is the single most common way a portfolio company damages itself with good intentions.

Anything that needs the warehouse built first. Sometimes genuinely necessary. Almost never the place to start, and frequently a way of deferring the hard conversation about what would actually be done with it.

The platform with AI attached. Vendors are adding capability to the systems your companies already run. Riding their roadmap captures most of the value of "new software" at none of the migration cost. Buy where the vendors are close; build only where the advantage is proprietary.

Across the book

The value compounds when you run it more than once.

A single assessment tells a management team what to do. Running the same assessment in three or four companies tells you something more useful: where the portfolio's real operating upside is concentrated, which opportunities recur across companies and could be built once, and — not least — which management teams can actually execute.

Because the method and the scoring are identical each time, the outputs are comparable. You can rank opportunities across the book rather than within one company, and put capital and attention where the return is, instead of where the loudest CEO is.

How it runs

  • Two to three weeks per company. Interviews with leadership and with the people doing the work, a process and systems map, and a review of what the company has already tried.
  • Every opportunity scored on value, cost, complexity, risk, and time to impact, then tiered — with the reasoning shown so your team can disagree with it.
  • A ninety-day path with named owners and one success metric per initiative.
  • Fixed fee and fixed timeline, agreed before we start.

Afterwards

We stay until it works.

The pattern that works is a single outside program driver — part-time but consistent — who runs the pilots, builds or configures the tools, manages the vendors, and reports on a fixed cadence to you and to the CEO. Part-time and consistent beats full-time and episodic, and both beat a plan handed to a team with no capacity to run it.

We stay until each initiative is live and a named person inside the company has taken it over. Where a portfolio company can run it themselves we will say so and hand it across, but that is the exception rather than the assumption.

Co-investment

Where we like a business enough to put our own money in, we will — and we are equally happy to be the operating partner on a deal that is yours. We invest our own capital, deal by deal, with no fund and no clock. We are not competing with you: our own investing sits well below institutional scale.

On live deals

We do occasionally look at a target during diligence — whether the AI narrative in the CIM is real, and what operating upside is genuinely available post-close. It is the same work against a shorter clock. Ask if it is useful, though it is not what most funds come to us for.
