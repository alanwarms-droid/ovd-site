For private equity

Every fund knows the portfolio has to move on this. The questions are what, when, and how much.

There is real urgency here and it is well founded. Hold periods are finite, the investment committee has asked, and a company that moves late on something this cheap to adopt gives ground it does not get back. The risk is not inaction. It is spending a year moving quickly in a direction that returns nothing.

The position

Conviction is settled. Sequencing is not.

Every management team in the portfolio can name a dozen things it could do, and most of them are individually plausible. What no one in the chain is positioned to supply is the ranking — which of the twelve is worth funding first, what each is worth in dollars, and how much the company can absorb at once without breaking something that works.

Each party answers a slightly different question. Management proposes what management wishes to build. Vendors scope against their own product. Operating partners are generalists asked to adjudicate a technical question, and the frameworks available to them assess maturity rather than return.

The useful output is not a strategy. It is a ranked, costed schedule of work that starts on Monday, with the largest and fastest dollar return at the top.

How initiatives are ranked

Three axes, applied to every candidate.

The same three in every company, which is what makes the results comparable across a book.

  • Dollar return What the initiative is worth in the accounts, and when. Timing is inside the number rather than beside it: a return arriving in month four is worth materially more than the same return in year three, and the discount is arithmetic rather than a matter of taste. Hours saved are converted to dollars or they are not counted.
  • Cost and effort The whole cost, not the licensing line. Integration nobody scoped, the exceptions that turn out to be half the volume, the training, the weeks of adoption before anything is running properly. This is the number vendors and internal advocates systematically understate, and estimating it honestly is much of the value of the exercise.
  • Organizational capacity What this company can actually absorb at once, given who is available and what else is underway. A portfolio company running three initiatives well will finish ahead of one running nine badly, and capacity — not ambition or budget — is what sets the number.

Scored the same way each time, so opportunities rank across the whole portfolio rather than within a single holding, and capital and attention follow the return.

The underlying decision

The question is what it has always been: make or buy.

AI has made it an order of magnitude easier to automate a business process. It is easier to build something bespoke, and it is simultaneously easier for software vendors to advance their own roadmaps. Both sides of the decision moved at once.

Before AI, 'make' was expensive and daunting. The cost has come down substantially, though it still requires a build mindset and a plan to maintain what gets built. What it now offers is the avoidance of a migration: not a project to uproot existing systems, but surgical strikes to capture value where it sits.

'Buy' brings support, certainty, and a roadmap shaped by many customers — along with the cost that those requirements may not match this company's, the integration effort, and lock-in that is difficult to reverse.

There are pluses and minuses on both sides, and the answer turns on cost, benefit, organizational capability, and alignment with what the business is actually defensible at. That is a familiar decision, made by people who have made it before.

Speed

A good deal of this can be done quickly.

The most valuable finding in most engagements is how much is available in weeks rather than quarters. Work that repeats hundreds of times a month, on information the business already holds, with a person retained to approve the output — that ships fast, and it compounds while the larger items are still being scoped.

The pattern holds because the constraint in a mid-market company is rarely capability. It is attention. An initiative that returns inside a quarter keeps its sponsor engaged and earns the right to the next one; an initiative measured at eighteen months loses its owner somewhere around month five.

Where a portfolio company has no one with the hours, OVD Advisory runs the work directly — pilots, tooling, vendors, reporting on a fixed cadence — until each initiative is live and a named person inside has taken it over.

Conduct

Two to three weeks per company.

  • Interviews with leadership and with the people performing the work, a process and systems map, and a review of what has already been attempted internally.
  • Every candidate scored on dollar return, cost and effort, and organizational capacity, then tiered, with the reasoning set out so that it can be examined.
  • A ninety-day plan with named owners and a single success measure for each initiative.
  • Fixed fee and fixed timetable, agreed in advance.
